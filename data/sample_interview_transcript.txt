Interviewer: You mentioned it got real in October. What has been the hardest part of adjusting to college?

Respondent: The money stuff, if I'm being honest. Everybody warns you about the tuition but it's the little things that get you. My laptop died the second week and that was six hundred dollars I did not have. Books were another three hundred. And I'm working twenty hours a week at the campus bookstore, which is supposed to be the manageable option, but twenty hours is twenty hours, you know? There were weeks I chose between picking up a shift and studying for a quiz, and I usually picked the shift because rent doesn't care about my quiz.

Interviewer: Can you say more about how that affected your classes?

Respondent: Yeah, so, my stats professor does these weekly quizzes, and my average tanked in the middle of the semester because of exactly that. I never told her why. Looking back I should have. But the hardest part, the actual hardest part, is the feeling that you're doing college on hard mode while some people are just... doing college. My roommate doesn't work. His parents pay for everything. He's a nice guy, we get along, but he genuinely did not know that financial aid could just change in the middle of a semester. Mine did. Nobody explained why, I just got an email saying my package was adjusted and I owed four hundred more dollars.

Interviewer: That sounds stressful.

Respondent: It was. The financial aid office people are nice enough one-on-one but you never get the same person twice. You explain your whole situation, they say they'll look into it, and then next time it's someone new and you start over. If I could change one thing about that office it would be, give me one person. Like a caseworker. Someone who knows my file.

Interviewer: I want to come back to changes later. For now, where do you feel most supported or most connected on campus?

Respondent: Hmm. Two places, honestly. The bookstore, weirdly. My coworkers are mostly other students in the same boat, working, commuting, juggling. We cover each other's shifts during finals. My manager is a student too, a senior, and she's basically been my unofficial advisor. She told me which professors to take, how to appeal a parking ticket, where the free food is on Thursdays. Half of what I know about surviving here came from her, not from orientation.

Interviewer: Mm-hmm.

Respondent: And the second one is this first-gen student group that meets every other Tuesday. I almost didn't go, because the flyer looked kind of sad, no offense to whoever made it. Nobody there thinks it's weird that I send money home some months, or that my mom keeps asking when I'm getting my degree in nursing when I'm not even in nursing. First meeting, this junior described the exact feeling I'd had all semester, like everyone else got a manual for college that you never received, and I was like, okay, I'm not broken, this is a thing. Respondent: Mostly by accident, which is kind of the problem. The tutoring center I found because I followed a friend there. Interviewer: How do you manage everything now? What does a normal week look like for you?

Respondent: Tighter than it used to be. I sleep more than I did in the fall, which sounds backwards, but tired me reads a page four times and remembers nothing, so sleep is actually efficient. And I say no to stuff now. There's a lot of fun stuff happening on this campus at exactly the times I cannot afford to do fun stuff.

Interviewer: Mm-hmm.

Respondent: The commute is still the thing I can't optimize. Forty minutes each way on a good day, and my schedule this semester has this evil three-hour gap on Tuesdays and Thursdays that's too short to go home and too long to do nothing. Interviewer: Last big question. If you could change one thing about your experience here, what would it be?

Respondent: One thing. Okay, the honest answer is money, make it cost less, but that's not a real answer, everyone says that. So the real answer, the achievable one, is: tell people about things before they need them. Like, orientation is a firehose of information in August when you don't know what any of it means. Nobody retains the counseling center wait times or the emergency fund application in August. So, I don't know what the fix looks like exactly. Maybe an email in week six, here's the stuff people actually need right now.
